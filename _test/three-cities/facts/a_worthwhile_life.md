# A life should be full and worthwhile

**Id:** `a_worthwhile_life` · **Added:** 2026-07-31

Everyone wants their life to have been worth living. Nobody is neutral about this, nobody has to be
persuaded of it, and nobody arrives at it by reasoning. It is the impulse underneath every other
motive a character has.

## What the character knows

- That they want their life to amount to something, and would be pained to find it hadn't.
- That this is true of everyone else too — including people whose idea of a worthwhile life is
  nothing like their own.

## What the fact deliberately does not say

**What "worthwhile" means.** That is the whole point. The impulse is universal; the *criterion* —
what actually counts as a life well spent — is not. It is inherited, learned, argued over, and
occasionally lost. Two characters in identical circumstances with identical knowledge will act
differently because they carry different answers to this question, and those answers came from
somewhere: a story about a specific person, a place, an event, a practice, read as an example of how
to live or how not to.

So this fact establishes only the *pull*. See `criterion` in `_lore/characters/<key>.json` and the
derivation procedure in `.claude/skills/character/SKILL.md` for where a character's particular answer
comes from, how it is derived from the collision of their knowledge with their backstory, and what
can change it.

## Why it is a fact and not lore

Because wanting one's life to matter isn't a doctrine anyone in this world could be unaware of — it
is what having a life is like. What *is* lore, and therefore sampled, contestable, and mutable, is
every specific idea about what makes a life good. Those travel as claims, take hold when they collide
with a life, and can be refuted. This cannot.

## What it does

It is the engine. Paired with [`life_is_finite`](life_is_finite.md), it is the will to live: time is
short and it matters what the time was spent on. Everything else in the model — the criterion, its
anchor, shocks and drift, the cost a character accrues by honoring their standard — is machinery for
answering the question this fact makes unavoidable, over and over, as circumstances change.

Notably, it is also what makes a broken criterion a crisis rather than a shrug. A character who loses
the story they were living by does not stop wanting their life to be worthwhile — they just no longer
know what that would look like. That gap is the most persuadable a person ever gets, and it is where
someone else's answer can take hold.
